    **Definition of Limits**

    Suppose $f : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ is a function defined on some
    interval containing $x = a \in \mathbb R$ (except possibly at $x=a$). We
    say that the limit of $f$ as $x$ approaches $a$ is $L \in \mathbb R$ and
    write
    $$
    \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L
    $$
    if for every $\epsilon \in \mathbb R^+$ there exists some
    $\delta \in \mathbb R^+$ such that
    $$
    0 < \abs{x - a} < \delta
    \qquad\text{implies}\qquad
    \abs{f(x) - L} < \epsilon
    $$
    If there is no $L \in \mathbb R$ such that the above holds, then we say
    the limit does not exist, and write
    $$
      \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = \text{DNE}
    $$

    <Accordion>
      <AccordionSummary expandIcon={<ExpandMoreIcon />}>
        <Typography variant="button">Intuition</Typography>
      </AccordionSummary>
      <AccordionDetails>
        We can imagine $\epsilon$ to represent some amount of maximum change in
        the vertical axis of the function $f$ and $\delta$ to represent an
        amount of change in the horizontal axis. Given $\epsilon$, we must find
        some small enough interval around $a$ such that $f(x)$ does not deviate
        from $L$ by more than $\epsilon$.

        If the function is "smooth", then picking smaller and smaller $\delta$'s
        makes the vertical deviation away from $L$ smaller and smaller in that
        interval. Eventually, we will find a $\delta$ that satisfies the
        condition in the definition.

        However, suppose $f$ has a jump discontinuity at $a$. Then in **any**
        interval around $a$, and for **any** constant $L$, the function deviates
        at least half the amount of the jump in the function from $L$. This in
        turn implies that such functions do not have a limit at $a$.
      </AccordionDetails>
    </Accordion>
    <Accordion>
      <AccordionSummary expandIcon={<ExpandMoreIcon />}>
        <Typography variant="button">Example</Typography>
      </AccordionSummary>
      <AccordionDetails>
        Consider a function $f : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ that is 1 everywhere
        except for at $x = 0$, where it is $2$.
        $$
          f(x) = \begin{cases}2 & x = 0 \\ 1 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}
        $$
        We show that
        $$
          \lim_{x \to 0} f(x) = 1
        $$
        First, consider an arbitrary $\epsilon \in \mathbb R^+$. We pick $\delta
        = 1$. Note that for any $x \in (-\delta, \delta)$ other than $x = 0$, we
        have $f(x) = 1$. Note that $\abs{f(x) - 1} = 0 < \epsilon$ as required.
      </AccordionDetails>
    </Accordion>
    <Accordion>
      <AccordionSummary expandIcon={<ExpandMoreIcon />}>
        <Typography variant="button">Other Limits</Typography>
      </AccordionSummary>
      <AccordionDetails>
        The definition of the limit given above is rather restrictive &ndash;
        it only permits functions mapping real numbers to real numbers. However,
        limits appear in many other areas of mathematics involving functions
        mapping between all sorts of spaces. In fact, some spaces may not have
        a convenient distance operator that can take the place of subtraction
        in $\mathbb R$. In these cases, different definitions of the limit are
        required.
      </AccordionDetails>

    **Definition of Continuity**

    A function $f : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ is *continuous* at a *point*
    $a \in \mathbb R$ if
    $$
      \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)
    $$
    A function is continuous in an *open interval* $(a, b)$ if it is
    continous at all points in $(a, b)$

    A function is *continuous everywhere* if it is continous at all points
    in its domain.

**Properties of Limits**

Suppose $f, g : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ are functions defined in a region
around $a \in \mathbb R$. Further let $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L_f$ and
$\lim_{x \to a} g(x) = L_g$. The following properties hold.

$$
\begin{align}
&\lim_{x \to a}c \cdot f(x) &=& \, c \cdot \lim_{x \to a} f(x) \\
&\lim_{x \to a} \left[f(x) + g(x)\right] &=& \lim_{x \to a} f(x) + \lim_{x \to a} g(x) \\
&\lim_{x \to a} \left[f(x) \cdot g(x)\right] &=& \lim_{x \to a} f(x) \cdot \lim_{x \to a} g(x) \\
&\lim_{x \to a} f^b(x) &=& \left[\lim_{x \to a} f(x)\right]^b
\end{align}
$$

        **Lemma:** Constant functions $f(x) = c$ are continuous everywhere

        <Divider />

        **Lemma:** Linear functions $f(x) = mx + b$ for $m, b \in \mathbb R$ are
        continuous everywhere.

        **Proof:** Let $a \in \mathbb R$ and $\epsilon \in \mathbb R^+$ be
        arbitrary. Pick $\delta = \frac{\epsilon}{2m}$ and $L = ma+b$. On the
        interval  $(a - \delta, a + \delta)$, we have $f(x) = f(a) + m(x-a)$.
        Then  $\abs{f(x) - L} = \abs{m(x-a)} \leq \abs{m\delta} \leq \frac{
        \epsilon}2 < \epsilon$ as required.

        polynomial.

  **Definition of the Derivative**

  Let $f : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ be a function. For a point $x \in \mathbb R$,
  we define the *derivative* of $f$ at $x$ to be

  $$
    f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}
  $$

  where the limit exists. This defines a function $f' : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$
  whose domain consists of the points $x \in \mathbb R$ where the above limit
  exists.
